Engineering that feels extraterrestrial.

I rarely post about things outside my immediate scope of work or leadership, but every now and then, you come across a piece of technology that makes you stop and appreciate what human engineering is capable of. ASML's EUV lithography machines are definitely one of those things.

It's hard not to be amazed by what goes into these machines. The more I learn about the technologies behind them, the closer they seem to something extraterrestrial!

These machines don't print 3D objects all at once, as one might imagine. Instead, they project microscopic circuit patterns onto a silicon wafer, layer by layer, to create the structures that eventually become complex microchips.

They use extreme ultraviolet (EUV) light, with a wavelength of just 13.5 nanometers. Conventional lithography light has a much longer wavelength, making it harder to achieve the same level of miniaturization. But the way they generate EUV light is almost science fiction: firing powerful lasers at microscopic droplets of molten tin, 50,000 times per second, creating plasma hotter than the surface of the Sun.

The resulting light is then directed through an incredibly sophisticated system of mirrors inside a vacuum chamber. These mirrors are manufactured by ZEISS, the sole supplier of the projection optics for ASML's EUV systems. And the mirrors themselves are a miracle of engineering. Their surface precision is so extreme that if you scaled one of them to the geographical size of Germany, the tallest bump on its surface would be approximately one millimeter high.

The precision doesn't stop there. The latest High-NA generation can resolve features down to approximately 8 nanometers, while the wafer positioning system operates with sub-nanometer precision.

The entire machine is the result of decades of research and collaboration across physics, optics, materials science, robotics and software engineering.

It's a great reminder that behind the AI revolution, there is an incredible amount of engineering happening far beyond algorithms, GPUs and data centers.
